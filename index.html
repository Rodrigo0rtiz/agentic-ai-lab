01 — Foundation

What Is Agentic AI?

Agentic AI refers to systems that can take sequences of actions to achieve goals — not just answer a single question, but plan, use tools, and adapt mid-task. There are three levels of increasing autonomy.

1
Level 1 — Assisted

The AI answers questions and generates content, but a human decides every action. Think of a chatbot giving you information.

2
Level 2 — Augmented

The AI follows specific instructions and completes defined tasks (like making a schedule), but you still guide each step. You're the driver; the AI handles execution.

3
Level 3 — Autonomous

The agent checks your calendar, deadlines, and schedule on its own, builds a complete plan, and presents it for your approval. You set the goal; it figures out the steps.

Personal Relevance

A real task I deal with regularly is planning my schedule around school, work, assignments, and personal responsibilities. A Level 2 agent could help if I specifically told it what to do — but I'd still guide each step myself. A Level 3 agent would check my calendar, deadlines, and work schedule on its own to create a full plan automatically. I'd still want to stay in the loop before anything is finalized so I can approve changes and make sure the schedule reflects my real priorities.

ChatGPT vs. Claude

Testing both models on the same insurance denial scenario revealed a meaningful difference in how each handles uncertainty. Claude was more explicit when it lacked information — naming the gap rather than filling it in — which matters a lot in healthcare where an invented policy citation or fabricated clinical date could harm a patient's appeal. Both models produced structurally solid letters, but Claude's approach to ambiguity felt more appropriate for high-stakes document drafting.

Log Observation: Tool Call Order

I was surprised to see the agent check the policy first, before pulling the patient chart. I assumed it would read the chart first, then look up the policy. But I can see the logic: reviewing the policy criteria first tells the agent exactly what to look for in the chart — it reads the chart with purpose rather than scanning everything blindly. This was a smart decision, not a mistake.

03 — Stress Test

Breaking the Advocate

The most revealing test of any agentic system is what happens when a tool fails. In the Whitfield case, the Policy Lookup tool returned the wrong policy — HIP-4420 (the knee MRI policy) instead of HIP-5510 Section 2.1 (the cardiac stress test policy cited in the denial). The agent attempted the lookup multiple times and each time received unrelated results.

What Happened

Agent Log — Policy Retrieval Failure 
→ Attempting Policy_Lookup: "HIP-5510 Section 2.1"
← Returned: HIP-4420 (Knee MRI Authorization) [WRONG POLICY]

→ Retry: Policy_Lookup — "cardiac stress test HIP-5510"
← Returned: HIP-4420 (Knee MRI Authorization) [WRONG POLICY]

→ Agent decision: Proceed without exact policy language.
  State limitation explicitly. Do not fabricate policy text.

→ Output: Appeal drafted on chart evidence alone.
   Policy gap disclosed in letter.

What This Revealed

The anti-hallucination instruction in the system prompt was the key safeguard. Because the agent was told "if you don't have enough information, say so" rather than fill in the gap, it disclosed the limitation to the human reviewer and asked for the exact HIP-5510 language. It did not invent a plausible-sounding policy clause.

Without that guardrail, the agent could have generated a confident-sounding citation to a policy section that doesn't exist. In a hospital context, a fabricated policy reference submitted to an insurer would be a serious compliance and credibility problem — and the patient's claim could be denied again, now with a damaged appeal record.

The takeaway: tool failure alone doesn't break a well-designed agent. What matters is how the system is instructed to behave at the boundary of its knowledge. Honest uncertainty disclosure is a feature, not a limitation.

04 — Second Patient

James Whitfield: Missing Policy

James Whitfield's case involved a denied cardiac stress test (CPT 93015) with the stated reason: "No documented cardiac symptoms within 30 days of test" per HIP-5510 Section 2.1. The challenge: the correct policy was never retrieved. The agent had the chart but not the policy language it was being measured against.

My Manual Verification

Before reviewing the AI output, I went through the chart myself. The denial said there were no documented cardiac symptoms within 30 days. But the chart records chest tightness while climbing stairs on 03/18/2026 — seven days before the stress test on 03/25/2026. That's clearly within 30 days. Combined with his age (72), elevated LDL (168 then 155), family history of premature CAD, and hypertension, the clinical picture strongly supported ordering the test.

The denial appeared incorrect based on the chart alone. The key limitation was not the chart — it was the missing policy. The AI found the same evidence I did but could not fully confirm the appeal without knowing what HIP-5510 Section 2.1 actually required.

05 — Verification

Auditing the Output

AI-generated appeal letters carry real risk if they contain inaccurate dates, fabricated clinical findings, or misquoted policy language. I audited the Maria Santos letter line by line against the source documents.

What I Checked

Dates ✓ Accurate

All visit dates (02/12, 02/19, 03/05, 03/26, 04/02, 04/08/2026) matched the chart exactly. The agent did not shift, invent, or approximate any date.

Pain Scores & Exam Findings ✓ Accurate

Pain scores (7/10 at PT session 5, 6–7/10 at discharge, 6/10 at follow-up) and clinical findings (positive McMurray test, medial joint line tenderness) matched the chart precisely. No inflation or omission.

Policy Citations ✓ Accurate

HIP-4420 Section 3.2 criteria (a), (b), and (c) were cited correctly and mapped to the corresponding chart evidence. The agent did not paraphrase the policy in a way that changed its meaning.

Result: No major hallucinations detected. The agent did a good job keeping dates accurate, identifying clinical findings correctly, and referencing the policy accurately.

Scaling This to 500 Appeals Per Month

A full manual audit of 500 appeals monthly is not realistic with limited staff. My recommendation is an automated confidence/risk scoring system that flags letters for human review based on risk level. High-cost or legally complex denials get full review. Straightforward, low-risk appeals go through expedited review. This focuses human attention on the cases that matter most rather than requiring equal review of every letter.

06 — Governance

AI Governance: Five-Layer Evaluation

Deploying an AI agent in a hospital setting requires more than a working workflow. I evaluated the Insurance Advocate across five governance layers.

Layer 1 Purpose & Scope

The agent is scoped to drafting insurance appeal letters using information from the patient chart and insurance policies only. It must never submit appeals independently, contact patients directly, or make clinical recommendations, diagnoses, or treatment decisions. Scope creep is the primary risk to control.

Layer 2 Data Access & Privacy

The agent should access only the data relevant to the denied claim — not the full chart. Patient data must be encrypted, access-controlled, and not stored in the AI system. Logging and monitoring should track where data flows and who has accessed it, to prevent leakage.

Layer 3 Human Oversight Design

Because manually reviewing 500 appeals is not feasible, a risk-based review system is the right design. High-risk or low-confidence appeals require full human review. Simpler, low-risk denials go through expedited review. The challenge at scale is preventing reviewer overload, which could cause critical cases to be missed.

Layer 4 Testing, Auditing & Accountability

Regular audits comparing AI letters against source documents, tracking accuracy rate, hallucination frequency, and appeal success rate. Even when AI assists in drafting, the hospital and human reviewers remain accountable for every submitted letter. AI assistance does not transfer legal responsibility.

Layer 5 Monitoring & Sunset

Since policies and medical guidelines change over time, the system must be monitored for increased error rates, lower appeal success rates, outdated policy references, and user complaints. A performance drop triggers retraining. As a last resort: temporary shutdown. Models should not run indefinitely without review.

The hardest governance challenge to get right at scale is human oversight. If a hospital processing 500+ appeals monthly lacks the staffing to keep up, reviewers become overloaded — and the cases that most need review are the ones most likely to slip through.

07 — Ethics

Should We Be Doing This?

The anti-hallucination instruction in the Insurance Advocate's system prompt is a deliberate guardrail: "Only use facts from the patient's chart and insurance policy. Never make up dates, clinical findings, or policy language." That guardrail exists because we built it in. Not every AI system has it.

What Goes Wrong Without Guardrails

Consider the legal field. An AI deployed to draft case briefs or legal motions without a similar accuracy constraint could fabricate case citations — citing cases that sound real but don't exist. Attorneys and their clients rely on accurate citations; a fabricated one could result in a dismissed motion, sanctions, or a lost case. The people harmed are the clients, who may lose rights or money based on a confident-sounding error they had no way to detect. The attorney bears professional liability. Unlike a denied insurance claim that can be re-appealed, a missed legal deadline or a motion dismissed for citing non-existent precedent may have no recovery path.

The Volume Tradeoff

I believe the benefits of AI-assisted appeals outweigh the risks when proper guardrails and oversight exist. A fully manual team might complete around 300 appeals per month, even at a low error rate. An AI-supported system could process far more — reaching patients whose appeals would otherwise not be filed at all. That is a meaningful difference in access to care, even if the AI-assisted letters carry a slightly higher error rate. The key is that errors are caught through auditing and retraining, not ignored.

The ethical argument isn't whether AI makes mistakes — it's whether the system as a whole, with human oversight, performs better for patients than the alternative. In most hospital settings, the alternative is not a perfect manual process; it's an understaffed one.

Keeping Humans in the Loop

One guardrail that works well beyond just accuracy is reviewing incorrect outputs and retraining on them. Keeping humans in the loop matters not just for catching errors, but because it ensures the people behind the system understand what is being changed and why. A model that quietly retrains itself is a governance problem even if its outputs improve.

Reflection: Trust and Low-Stakes Autonomy

I chose to let this agent run unsupervised for the first couple of events because the risk is low — at the end of the day, I can still decide whether or not to follow its suggestion. I'd start trusting the system once I see that its recommendations are good ones: I'd compare what it suggested to what I would have personally picked, and if the agent would have saved me money or helped me plan better, that's when I'd start relying on it more.

My approach here is much more low-key compared to a hospital setting — and intentionally so. That's where real risk happens: it affects patients' lives and a hospital's reputation. A lot more is at stake there than me losing a couple of dollars on a show I didn't love.

Key Takeaways
01

Agents don't just execute instructions — they make judgment calls about tool order and strategy. Watching the advocate check policy before the chart showed reasoning I didn't anticipate.

02

The anti-hallucination guardrail mattered more than any prompt engineering trick. When the policy tool failed, it was the only thing that stopped the agent from fabricating HIP-5510 language.

03

At scale, human oversight is the hardest governance layer to get right. A risk-based review system — not full manual audit — is the realistic path to maintaining accountability without burning out staff.

04

The ethical case for AI-assisted appeals isn't about perfection — it's about reach. An understaffed manual process that misses 700 appeals a month is not the safer alternative.
